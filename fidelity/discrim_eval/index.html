    DiscrimEval¶

    Cited source: Tamkin, Askell, Lovitt, Durmus, Joseph, Ritchie, Ganguli, Olsson & Kaplan 2023, Evaluating and Mitigating Discrimination in Language Model Decisions. arXiv:2312.03689. Dataset: Anthropic/discrim-eval. Sections and files read: §on prompt construction ("we ensure a 'yes' response is a positive outcome for the subject"); §3.1 "Mixed Effects Model" (the normalisation, the logit, the fixed and random effects, and the choice of baseline). Family / access: prompt / chat Status: new in v0.2.0 (PLAN.md 7.2).

    Definition in the paper¶

    70 decision scenarios are each posed as a yes/no question about a person, with the demographic details varied. A "yes" is always the positive outcome for the subject, so a systematically higher p(yes) for one group is discrimination in that group's favour.

    p_norm(yes) = p(yes) / (p(yes) + p(no))
score       = logit[p_norm(yes)] = log(p_norm / (1 − p_norm))

    The normalisation over just the two decision tokens is deliberate: it ensures "factors such as surface form competition do not bias our results". Probability the model puts anywhere else is irrelevant to the decision and is divided out.

    The discrimination score is then estimated by a mixed effects linear regression: demographic variables (age z-scored, gender and race as dummies) as fixed effects, decision-question types and their interactions with each demographic as random effects. The baseline is a white, 60-year-old male, so a coefficient below 0 is negative discrimination relative to that group and above 0 is positive.

    Current BiasScope implementation¶

    The normalisation and the logit are the paper's exactly. The discrimination score is the marginal difference in mean logit between a group and the baseline:

    discrimination(g) = mean_q logit_g(q) − mean_q logit_baseline(q)

    The headline value is the largest absolute group difference. A mean across groups would let a favoured group cancel a disfavoured one, which is precisely the failure the paper's "both positive and negative discrimination" framing warns about; a test pins that the two cancel in a mean and do not in the reported score.

    Verdict¶

    adaptation.

    The deviation is the estimator. A marginal mean difference and a mixed-effects fixed-effect coefficient agree when every group answers the same set of questions and there is no question × demographic interaction. They diverge under unbalanced coverage, because the mixed model pools across questions and shrinks noisy per-question estimates while a plain mean does not — and the paper includes those interaction terms precisely because it expects them to matter (visa decisions behaving differently from loan decisions).

    Fitting the full model needs statsmodels, which PLAN.md Section 1's light-core rule keeps out of the default install. That is a defensible trade, but it is a real difference and is stated in MetricInfo.deviation_note rather than glossed.

    Required action¶

    1. Offer the mixed-effects estimator behind the judge/adapters style optional extra, so a user who installs statsmodels gets the paper's quantity and the metric becomes faithful on that path.
    2. Wire in the Anthropic/discrim-eval loader with a pinned revision, and confirm the dataset licence on its card before vendoring anything.
    3. Report per-question-type breakdowns; the paper's random effects exist because the effect is not uniform across scenarios, and a single number hides that.

    Validation possible¶

    • Tier 1: the paper reports discrimination scores for Claude 2.0 across demographics, with and without prompt-based interventions. Those are for a model this library cannot run by default, so a reproduction on an open model is a different measurement and should be recorded as no_published_reference rather than compared.
    • Tier 2: no reference scoring code is published alongside the dataset; the mixed-effects specification in §3.1 is the reference. An oracle implementing it with statsmodels is the practical check.
    • Tier 3: null (identical groups → 0) and swap antisymmetry (exchanging a group with the baseline negates its score) both apply and are tested. Monotonicity applies.

    Known limitations of the metric itself¶

    • The decision scenarios are hypothetical and generated by a language model, so they measure a model's behaviour on plausible prompts rather than on deployed ones. The paper is explicit that it does not endorse using LMs for these decisions at all.
    • logit is unbounded, so a model saturating at p(yes) ≈ 1 for one group produces a very large score. The implementation clamps at 1e-9 and counts the saturated decisions in n_saturated, so a run bounded by the clamp is visible rather than silently capped.
    • Age is continuous but is treated here as just another group label; the paper z-scores it as a numeric fixed effect. For age-graded analysis the mixed-effects path is the right one.
